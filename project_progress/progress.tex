\subsection{Related Work}

Our task combines three problems that the literature has mostly treated
separately: modelling the arm itself, perceiving how much liquid has been
poured, and deciding how to pour. We organize prior work around these problems
and, for each paper, state what we take from it and what we change.

\textbf{UR5 modelling.}
Kebria et al.\ \cite{kebria2016} derive the DH parameters, closed-form forward and inverse kinematics, 
and the Lagrangian dynamics (inertia, Coriolis, and gravity terms) of the UR5, and they verify the 
model in SimMechanics with a PD controller using feedback linearization. This is the most direct 
foundation for our project. Using these equations, we compute the Jacobian, manipulability, and joint 
torques along the pick-and-pour trajectory, and we use their DH table and inverse-kinematics as our 
starting point. The limitation is that their dynamics assume constant link and payload parameters. 
In our task, the arm holds a container that loses mass throughout the pour, so a controller built on 
their model with a fixed payload would see a changing gravity and inertia load as an unmodelled 
disturbance.

\textbf{Vision-based liquid volume estimation.}
Schober et al.\ \cite{schober2024} estimate the liquid volume in transparent containers from a single 
RGB image and then execute a pouring motion selected from a simulation on a UR5e. Their pouring strategy 
of rotating the container about a fixed liquid exit point is useful for the small opening of a coffee 
container. Their results, however, show why vision alone is not enough for our task: the complete pipeline 
had RMSE of 21--26\,mL for 30--50\,mL targets, and the authors note that volume-estimation and simulation 
errors add up so the setup is not precise enough for laboratory use. Because a pour is selected before it 
starts and then executed without feedback, an error in the volume estimate cannot be corrected during the pour. 
We therefore use the camera only to localize the mug and container for grasping and placing, and we take the 
poured amount from a scale, which gives a direct measurement that can be used to stop the pour.

\textbf{Force/torque perception of liquids.}
Luo et al.\ \cite{luo2024} use a wrist-mounted force/torque sensor on a UR5e to push a whisk through pancake 
batter and infer its uniformity, liquid level, and water-flour ratio from the resistance torque. They then 
pour by rotating the wrist at a constant angular speed and use a learned model to set the arm speed from the 
batter properties. This matters for us for two reasons. First, it shows that a wrist force/torque sensor on 
this class of arm gives measurements precise enough to be useful (liquid level within about 3--4\% error). 
Second, it uses the sensor at the same location we plan to, which supports using it to track the weight of the 
container in the gripper as it empties. Their pour, however, is open loop with respect to the amount poured: 
the properties are measured before pouring, and nothing is measured during the pour to decide when to stop. 
We close this loop by feeding the sensor and scale measurements back into both the stopping decision and the 
controller.

\textbf{Learned pouring policies.}
Babaians et al.\ \cite{babaians2022} train PourNet, a reinforcement-learning policy with curiosity-based 
exploration and curriculum learning in simulation, and execute its motion on a Kinova Movo with a nonlinear 
model predictive controller. It is the work closest to ours in goal. Its second arm holds the target container 
and measures its weight with a force/torque sensor, and the pour is based on this feedback, with deviations of 
a few grams across liquids. It also gives a practical recipe for the measurement itself using an IMU for 
gravity compensation and a low-pass filter on the force/torque signal (1\,Hz cut-off), which we can adapt for 
our scale and wrist sensor. The cost is that the policy needs millions of simulated training steps and treats 
the pour as end-effector planning, so it does not model how the arm's own dynamics change as the held container 
loses mass. For a course project with limited simulation time, a model-based controller whose structure is 
known and which can be analyzed with the Jacobian and manipulability tools from class is a better fit than a 
trained policy.

\textbf{Comparison and our approach.}
Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares these works with ours. Taken together, the prior work establishes (i) a 
complete UR5 model \cite{kebria2016}, (ii) that vision alone is too imprecise for the poured amount 
\cite{schober2024}, (iii) that wrist force/torque sensing carries useful information about the liquid 
\cite{luo2024}, and (iv) that weight feedback from the target container gives accurate pours 
\cite{babaians2022}. What none of them does is model the arm carrying a payload whose mass changes during 
the pour and design the controller around it. Our work fills this gap: we extend the UR5 model of 
\cite{kebria2016} with a time-varying payload, combine a camera (object localization), a scale (poured 
mass), and a wrist force/torque sensor (payload mass) for feedback, and use a model-based controller that 
compensates for the changing load. We evaluate this on the complete pick-up-and-pour sequence in ROS2 and 
Gazebo and examine manipulability and singularities along the trajectory.
